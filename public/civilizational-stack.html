The framework

The civilizational stack.

What power is made of in the twenty-first century.

The decisive competition of this century is not over any single technology. It is over the connected system through which societies convert resources, knowledge, capital and human talent into capability.

Power is a conversion process

A civilization is not powerful merely because it possesses resources, scientists, capital or advanced technology. It is powerful when it can reliably convert them.

Energy must become industrial activity. Minerals must become strategic components. Chips must become compute. Compute must become intelligence. Intelligence must become machines. Machines must become scalable production. Production must become prosperity, infrastructure and defense. Institutions must keep the whole system lawful, adaptive and capable of renewal.

Power is not a static possession. It is a conversion process.

Across every layer: talent, capital, law, alliances and legitimacy.

Why a stack, not a supply chain

A supply chain explains how inputs move toward a product. The civilizational stack explains how capabilities depend on one another, where bottlenecks emerge, who controls the hardest-to-replace layers and whether a society can turn an advantage in one domain into power across the rest.

A frontier AI model appears to be software. Beneath it are chip-design tools, fabrication plants, specialized equipment, materials, data centres, electrical substations, cooling systems, networks, engineers, capital markets, law and political stability.

Modern prosperity hides its dependencies well. The final product can look weightless while the system underneath it is enormous.

Four tests of a civilizational stack

Stack conversion

How effectively does a society turn talent, capital, knowledge and political intention into usable capability?

Spending is an input. Capability is an outcome. A large infrastructure budget does not guarantee infrastructure. A large defense budget does not guarantee readiness. A large research base does not guarantee industrial scale.

Stack sovereignty

Can every essential capability be accessed, replaced or rebuilt under pressure?

Sovereignty does not require autarky. It requires the absence of coercive vulnerability: no hostile actor should be able to disable a critical function for longer than a society can withstand.

Stack debt

What inherited capability is being consumed faster than it is renewed?

Deferred maintenance, aging grids, depleted stockpiles, disappearing technical expertise and weakened public institutions may remain invisible for years. A crisis reveals the unpaid liability.

Stack renewal

Is the society reproducing the people, infrastructure, institutions and industrial knowledge on which future capability depends?

Civilizations decline not only when they stop inventing, but when they consume accumulated capability faster than they can replace it.

The bottleneck matters more than the average

A stack can be strong almost everywhere and still be strategically weak because of one hard constraint.

A model without compute does not train. A factory without reliable power does not produce. A mine without processing capacity does not create material sovereignty. A military without replacement munitions cannot endure.

The relevant question is not simply which society possesses the most assets. It is which society can identify and remove the bottleneck that prevents those assets from becoming outcomes.

Open stacks and closed stacks

Closed systems may coordinate quickly. They can direct credit, secure land, impose standards, tolerate overcapacity and pursue priorities over long periods. But the same concentration can suppress bad news, reward loyalty over competence and make catastrophic mistakes harder to correct.

Open systems are often better at experimentation, talent attraction, independent inquiry, trusted alliances and peaceful error correction. But they may struggle with fragmented authority, accumulated vetoes, short political horizons and the inability to coordinate investment.

Authoritarian systems may possess stronger coordination but weaker error correction. Open systems may possess stronger experimentation and error correction but weaker coordination.

The future may belong to whichever system best solves its own weakness.

The Open Civilization challenge

Open societies should not imitate closed systems in order to compete with them. They must learn to coordinate without suppressing experimentation, act without eliminating restraint, and build power without concentrating it beyond correction.

Strategic sovereignty does not mean national self-sufficiency. It means resilient, governable interdependence among societies capable of trusting one another under pressure.

The question is not whether open societies possess talent, capital or technology. It is whether their civilizational stack can convert those advantages into things that work, scale and endure.

The task is not to close the open world in order to strengthen it. It is to reconnect its civilizational stack: to restore the pathways through which freedom generates knowledge, knowledge becomes capability, capability produces prosperity and security, and successful institutions renew the freedom on which the entire system depends.
